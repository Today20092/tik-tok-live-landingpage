# Islam pamphlet research

## What U.S. surveys actually measure

Pew's Religious Landscape Study is the starting point for U.S. context. In the 2023–24 survey, roughly 1% of U.S. adults identified as Muslim. Buddhists and Hindus each accounted for roughly 1%, and roughly 2% identified religiously as Jewish. These are adult religious self-identification estimates. Jewish identity can also be cultural or ancestral. The study measures affiliation, beliefs and practices. Use Pew's separate knowledge survey for factual questions. [Religious Landscape Study](https://www.pewresearch.org/religious-landscape-study/)

Pew's February 2019 knowledge survey asked 10,971 U.S. adults 32 multiple-choice factual questions. They answered about 14 correctly on average. Results describe responses to particular items at the time of fieldwork. Question difficulty and coverage vary by religion, and Pew cautions against direct comparison with its earlier knowledge survey. The figures below are for U.S. adults overall. [2019 report and methods](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2019/07/23/what-americans-know-about-religion/)

| Question | Correct | Unsure | What people selected |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| Muslim share of U.S. adults | 26% chose under 5% | 57% | 12% chose about one in ten, 3% about one in four and 1% half or more. |
| Majority religion in Indonesia | 26% chose Islam | 43% | 16% chose Hinduism and 11% Buddhism, the most frequent wrong choices. |
| What Ramadan is | 60% chose an Islamic holy month | 30% | The alternative choices were a Jewish prayer for the dead (5%), a Hindu festival of lights (3%) and a celebration of Buddha's birth (1%). |
| Islam's holiest city and pilgrimage destination | 62% chose Mecca | 21% | 14% chose Jerusalem, 1% Medina and 1% Cairo. |

Shares are rounded and the table omits nonresponse. The Ramadan item measured recognition of the month. The pilgrimage question supplied the obligation and the ability qualifier before asking for the city. It therefore tells us little about whether people could independently explain fasting or pilgrimage. [Pew's original questionnaire and topline, items NUMREL, RELCOUNTRY, RMDN and PLGRMG](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2019/07/W44-Religious-Knowledge-TOPLINE-FOR-RELEASE-CHECK-COMPLETE.pdf)

The same survey found that 81% identified the Resurrection as what Easter commemorates and 79% identified the Christian doctrine of one God in three persons. In contrast, 29% knew that the Jewish Sabbath begins on Friday. On two other items, 18% recognized the truth of suffering as one of Buddhism's Four Noble Truths and 15% identified the Vedas with Hindu tradition. Familiar Christian terms can orient some readers. Define Jewish and other religious terms wherever they appear and avoid equating quiz performance with familiarity with an entire tradition. [Pew questionnaire and topline](https://www.pewresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/20/2019/07/W44-Religious-Knowledge-TOPLINE-FOR-RELEASE-CHECK-COMPLETE.pdf), [Pew's interpretation and limitations](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2019/07/23/what-americans-know-about-religion/)

### Attitudes and perceptions

- Pew's 2023 report found that 17% of U.S. adults expressed favorable views toward Muslims and 22% unfavorable views. Another 59% gave a neutral response or said they did not know enough to say. The published figure groups those two responses together. [Pew's favorability survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2023/03/15/americans-feel-more-positive-than-negative-about-jews-mainline-protestants-catholics/)
- In Pew's May 2026 survey, 42% said Muslim Americans were having a negative impact on the country and 43% described Muslims as less patriotic than other Americans. These are opinions and comparative judgments elicited by distinct questions. Use them to understand the audience context and avoid interpreting them as quiz errors or as changes in the 2023 favorability measure. [Pew's 2026 portrait and survey chart](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2026/09/03/25-years-after-9-11-a-portrait-of-muslim-americans/)

## Fundamentals and comparisons

- **One God, revelation and prophethood.** Islam is the religion and Muslims are its followers. The Qur'an teaches the oneness of God, called Allah in Arabic. Muslims understand the Qur'an as revelation from God and Muhammad as God's messenger and the final prophet. The profession of faith gives a compact way to introduce both ideas. [Qur'an 112](https://quran.com/112), [2:185](https://quran.com/2/185), [33:40](https://quran.com/33/40), [Sahih al-Bukhari 8](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:8)
- **Worship in daily life.** Introduce the Five Pillars in plain language first: profession of faith (shahada), ritual prayer (salat), obligatory giving (zakat), fasting in Ramadan (sawm) and pilgrimage to Mecca for those able to go (hajj). Ramadan is the month and fasting is a practice observed during it. The Qur'an provides accommodations for illness and travel. This framing describes religious obligations while leaving room for differences in observance. [Sahih al-Bukhari 8](https://sunnah.com/bukhari:8), [Qur'an 2:185](https://quran.com/2/185), [3:97](https://quran.com/3/97)
- **Judaism and Christianity.** The Qur'an names Abraham, Moses and Jesus among those associated with revelation. The Jewish Shema in Deuteronomy affirms God's oneness. In the Qur'an, Jesus is the Messiah, born to Mary, and a messenger of God. The Qur'an rejects his divinity and the Trinity. Mainstream Christianity confesses one God in three persons and places Jesus' death and Resurrection at the center of its proclamation. Judaism can be introduced in its own terms through the Torah and the Shema. Use one shared point and one clear difference, without implying that the traditions have interchangeable scriptures or beliefs. [Qur'an 2:136](https://quran.com/2/136), [3:45–47](https://quran.com/3/45-47), [4:171](https://quran.com/4/171), [Deuteronomy 6:4 in Chabad's Tanakh](https://www.chabad.org/library/bible_cdo/aid/9970/jewish/Chapter-6.htm), [1 Corinthians 15](https://bible.usccb.org/bible/1corinthians/15), [Vatican II on Islam and Judaism](https://www.vatican.va/archive/hist_councils/ii_vatican_council/documents/vat-ii_decl_19651028_nostra-aetate_en.html)
- **Other faiths.** The Buddhism and Hinduism results above help set the reading level. A wider survey of religions would use space needed for core Islamic concepts. A geographic example can show that a country may be home to multiple traditions, with Islam the majority religion in Indonesia and a minority religion in India. [Pew's 2020 global estimates, published in 2025](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2025/06/09/muslim-population-change/)

## Ranked misconceptions and knowledge gaps

This is an editorial ranking for limited panel space. It weighs the strength of direct measurement and how much the explanation helps a first-time reader. The answer distributions establish what respondents selected, and uncertainty stays separate from a specific wrong belief.

| Priority | What to address | Strength of evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Islam's geographic reach.** Start with Indonesia as a Muslim-majority country and mention India as home to a large Muslim minority. | Strong historical measurement of the Indonesia knowledge gap and its wrong answers. Pew's global estimates establish that the Asia-Pacific region is home to the greatest number of Muslims. The U.S. question does not measure the prevalence of equating Muslim identity with Arab ethnicity. |
| 2 | **Size of the U.S. Muslim population.** Give the dated RLS estimate, roughly 1% of U.S. adults, if a demographic figure fits. | Strong historical measurement of both uncertainty and explicit overestimates on this item. If space is tight, a U.S. example of ordinary Muslim life may be more helpful than reporting the quiz result. |
| 3 | **Mecca and hajj.** Explain Mecca's role, the purpose of pilgrimage as worship and the ability qualifier. | Strong historical evidence that some selected Jerusalem and others were unsure. The item supports a city correction, while the description of hajj comes from religious sources. |
| 4 | **Ramadan and fasting.** Connect a familiar word to the practice and explain accommodations briefly. | Strong historical evidence of a recognition gap. Wrong answers were spread across distractors and uncertainty was more frequent. |
| 5 | **Allah, Muhammad and Jesus.** Define Allah and explain whom Muslims worship and how Muhammad and Jesus are understood. | Strong textual support and high value for a beginner. The cited U.S. quiz supplies no current prevalence estimate for the claims that Allah is a separate deity, that Muslims worship Muhammad or that Islam has no place for Jesus. Avoid calling these widely held beliefs. |

## Six-panel editorial selection

| Panel | Content | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Cover | A plain title and a short invitation to learn what Muslims believe and practice. | Readers may have limited background or mixed views. A welcoming opening sets the subject without leading with polling or stereotypes. |
| 2. Beliefs | One God, the Qur'an and Muhammad's role as messenger. Define Islam, Muslim and Allah as the words arise. | These definitions make the rest of the pamphlet easier to read. Prioritize them because of their importance to the faith, independent of survey coverage. |
| 3. Practices | Introduce the Five Pillars. Give Ramadan and hajj a short explanation each. | These are the measured points of partial familiarity. The other pillars supply context for prayer and giving. |
| 4. Connections | Abraham and Moses, the Jewish affirmation of God's oneness, and Jesus and Mary in Islam. Describe the difference on Jesus' divinity in both traditions' own terms. | Familiar figures offer a way in, and one specific difference prevents a misleading impression of agreement. |
| 5. Muslim communities | Use Indonesia and India to make geographic diversity concrete. Add the dated U.S. population estimate or a brief U.S. example. | Covers the two lowest-scoring Islam-related items in the topline and makes room for U.S. Muslims as part of the audience's own society. |
| 6. Further reading | Provide a small set of readable links or a QR code with a printed destination. Include the Qur'an passages used and Pew's research. | Lets readers follow the religious and demographic claims back to their sources. |

Keep the quiz response shares and the attitudes figures in this research brief. The pamphlet can answer readers' likely questions directly, with the dated U.S. demographic figure as the main statistic. The companion draft moves U.S. context to the opening flap and uses the back for two short questions and a next step. It uses a 2017 Pew result about targeting civilians to respond to a concern documented in January 2026, when 51% of U.S. adults said Islam is more likely than other religions to encourage violence. In 2017, 76% of U.S. Muslims said targeting and killing civilians to advance a political, social, or religious cause is never justified. The two surveys asked different populations different questions. [Pew January 2026 survey results](https://www.pewresearch.org/chart/views-on-islam-are-now-more-negative-and-more-partisan-than-in-2002/), [Pew 2017 survey on targeting civilians](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2017/07/26/terrorism-and-concerns-about-extremism/). The selection reflects national survey findings and the structure of the topic. It has yet to be tested with pamphlet readers.
