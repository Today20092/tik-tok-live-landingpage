## What Muslims believe

### One God

_Islam_ is the religion. A _Muslim_ is someone who follows it.

At the center of Islam is belief in one God, with no partners, no children and nothing like Him. _Allah_ is the Arabic word for God. Arabic-speaking Christians use the same word in their Bibles.[^one-god]

### The prophets and one message

Muslims believe God sent many prophets, from Adam to Muhammad. Many of them appear in the Bible too, including Abraham, Moses, David and Jesus.

The Qur'an says that every messenger called people to worship God alone. Muslims affirm all the prophets God sent.[^prophets]

<PamphletFigure name="prophets" />

### The Qur'an

The Qur'an is the holy book of Islam. Muslims believe it is God's word, revealed to Muhammad over about 23 years.

Muslims also believe God gave earlier scriptures. These include the Torah to Moses, the Psalms to David and the Gospel to Jesus.[^quran]

### Muhammad

Muhammad was born in Mecca, in what is now Saudi Arabia. Muslims believe he was the last prophet. He was a human being, not a divine figure. The Qur'an calls him a messenger and identifies him as the last of the prophets.

After his name, Muslims often say or write ﷺ. It is short for "peace and blessings be upon him."[^muhammad]

<PamphletFigure name="muhammad" />

### The six articles of faith

The angel Gabriel (_Jibril_) once asked the Prophet what faith is. He answered with six beliefs, and Muslims still use that list today.[^articles]

<PamphletFigure name="articles" />

### The Five Pillars

The Five Pillars are the central practices of Islam.

The Qur'an makes room for people who can't do some of them. Someone who is ill or traveling can make up a missed fast later. The pilgrimage is required only of those who are able to go.[^pillars]

<PamphletFigure name="pillars" />

#### When Muslims pray

Each of the five daily prayers has its own time window.

In Pew's 2023–24 survey, 59% of U.S. Muslim adults said they pray several times a day. The survey counted informal prayer too, so it doesn't tell us how many people perform all five daily prayers.[^prayer-survey]

### The purpose of life

The Qur'an teaches that God created human beings and jinn to worship Him. In Islamic belief, jinn are ordinarily unseen beings.

In Islam, _worship_ (_'ibadah_) means more than ritual. Prayer and fasting count. So do honesty, mercy toward others and looking after your own health, when you do them for God. The Prophet ﷺ taught that God judges deeds by their intentions.[^purpose]

The Qur'an describes life and death as a test of who does best in their deeds. On the Day of Judgment, people will see even the smallest good or bad deed they did.[^test]

## Jesus and Mary in Islam

Muslims love and honor Jesus, called _Isa_ in Arabic. The Qur'an tells much of his story.

Mary (_Maryam_) has a whole chapter named after her, [Surah Maryam (chapter 19)](https://quran.com/19?translations=85).[^jesus]

<PamphletFigure name="jesus" />

Muslims see Jesus as a great prophet and the Messiah. They also see him as human, not God and not the Son of God. They believe God raised him up and that he was not crucified.

This is a real difference from mainstream Christian belief. Christianity confesses one God in three persons, and Jesus' death and Resurrection are at the center of its faith.[^jesus-difference]

## Questions people ask

### God and the Prophet

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Is Allah a different God?</summary>

No. _Allah_ is the Arabic word for God. Muslims believe He is the same God who spoke to Abraham, Moses and Jesus.

Arabic-speaking Christians also say Allah, and Arabic Bibles use it.[^one-god]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Do Muslims worship Muhammad?</summary>

No. Muslims worship God alone. They believe Muhammad received the Qur'an, and they look to his life as an example of how to practice Islam.

The Qur'an calls him a messenger and the final prophet, and it reminds readers that other messengers came before him. Muslims often say "peace be upon him" after his name out of respect.[^faq-muhammad]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>What do Sunni and Shia mean?</summary>

Sunni and Shia are two major traditions within Islam. Both believe in one God, the Qur'an, and Muhammad as God's messenger.

They split over who should lead the community after Muhammad's death.[^faq-sunni]

</details>

### Other faiths and society

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Does everyone have to become Muslim?</summary>

Muslims invite others to Islam. The Qur'an forbids compulsion in religion.

It calls for justice even toward enemies, and kindness toward people who are at peace with Muslims.[^others]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Can people of other faiths live and worship freely?</summary>

The Qur'an forbids compulsion in religion and tells Muslims to act justly even toward people they dislike. It calls for kindness and fairness toward those who have neither fought Muslims over their faith nor driven them out of their homes.

Jews, Christians and other communities lived and worshiped under many Muslim governments. Their rights and restrictions differed by place and period.

For example, an Ottoman reform edict in 1856 restated protections for religious communities and called for fair representation in their councils. The edict records what the government promised. It doesn't tell us how officials kept those promises.[^faq-freedom]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>What is Sharia, and what does it mean in the United States?</summary>

Sharia is Islamic guidance on how to live. It covers prayer, fasting, charity, business and family life. Muslim scholars call their work of interpreting it _fiqh_.

Interpretations differ. Some governments have written particular interpretations into their laws.

In the U.S., a Muslim might fast during Ramadan or give zakat as part of their faith. Federal and state law decide people's legal rights and duties. The First Amendment protects religious practice and bars the government from setting up a religion.[^faq-sharia]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Does being Muslim imply a political agenda for the United States?</summary>

No. Muslim Americans hold many different political views, and they have a long history here. In 1831, Omar Ibn Said, a Muslim scholar enslaved in North Carolina, wrote his autobiography in Arabic.

In Pew's 2017 survey of U.S. Muslims, 92% said they were proud to be American. A person's religion tells you little about their political goals.[^faq-politics]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>What do U.S. Muslims say about violence against civilians?</summary>

In a 2017 Pew survey, 76% of U.S. Muslims said targeting and killing civilians for a political, social or religious cause is never justified.

The Qur'an tells believers to act justly even toward a group they dislike. In an authentic hadith, the Prophet forbade the killing of women and children in war.[^faq-violence]

</details>

### Women and family

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>What rights does the Qur'an give women?</summary>

The Qur'an promises that God will never let the deeds of any of the faithful go to waste, whether they are men or women. It gives women a share of family inheritance and directs men to give the marriage gift to the bride herself. Inheritance shares depend on the family relationship and can differ between men and women.

The Qur'an tells men to give the marriage gift to the bride herself.

The Qur'an tells husbands to treat their wives honorably and permits a wife to seek release from marriage through _khulʿ_. An authentic hadith instructs that a woman be asked for her consent before marriage.

How women experience these rights varies between families and societies.[^faq-women]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Do all Muslim women wear hijab? What happens if they don't?</summary>

The Qur'an tells men and women to lower their gaze and guard their chastity. It also instructs women to draw their head coverings over their chests. Many Muslim women wear a headscarf, often called a hijab, as part of their faith. Others wear it sometimes or never, and Pew found all of these among U.S. Muslim women.

The Qur'an's passage on modesty sets no worldly penalty for going without a headscarf. Government rules and social pressure vary by country. A UN investigation found that Iranian authorities used violence to enforce Iran's compulsory hijab law.[^faq-hijab]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Does the Qur'an give a husband the right to beat his wife?</summary>

[Qur'an 4:34](https://quran.com/4/34?translations=85) deals with conflict between spouses. Many translations use the word "strike," and Muslim scholars disagree about what it allows.

Some classical scholars allowed a limited physical act and put restrictions on it. Others read the verse as nonviolent, or point out that the Prophet's example rules out striking a spouse. Aisha said that Muhammad never struck a woman. The next verse calls for mediators when a marriage is at risk of breaking down.

Egypt's Dar al-Ifta rejects using the verse to justify harming or humiliating a wife. Abuse is serious, and anyone facing it deserves safety and support.[^faq-434]

</details>

<details class="pamphlet-faq">
<summary>Did the Prophet marry a child?</summary>

In _Sahih al-Bukhari_, Aisha reports that the Prophet married her when she was six and that the marriage was consummated when she was nine. By today's ordinary use of the word, she was a child. The report describes her marriage and gives no general minimum age.

The Qur'an gives no numerical minimum age for marriage. [Qur'an 4:6](https://quran.com/4/6?translations=85) discusses when to return an orphan's property and mentions both marriageable age and sound judgment. Some Muslim scholars draw on it to distinguish physical puberty from maturity for marriage. Classical jurists discussed marriage contracts involving minors. Some distinguished the contract from when the couple could live together, taking physical readiness and risk of harm into account.

The International Islamic Fiqh Academy affirms that countries may set a marriage age to protect welfare, and its resolution calls for consent and safeguards. The UK's Ifta recommends 18 as the minimum even where local law permits an earlier marriage.[^faq-marriage]

</details>

## Muslims in the U.S. and around the world

About 2.0 billion people were Muslim in 2020. That is roughly 1 in 4 people worldwide (26%).

Islam was the fastest-growing major religion between 2010 and 2020.[^global]

In the U.S., 1.2% of adults identify as Muslim. No single racial or ethnic group makes up a majority of U.S. Muslims.[^us]

## Visiting a mosque

A mosque (_masjid_) is a place of prayer, and usually a community center too. In the U.S. many are called Islamic centers.

Many mosques welcome visitors. If you're unsure where to go or what to do, ask someone when you arrive.[^mosque]

- **Plan ahead.** You can [find mosques near you on a map](https://www.google.com/maps/search/mosque+near+me). Many list prayer times and opening hours online. It helps to call or email first. Some hold visiting hours or open houses.
- **Dress modestly.** Men and women usually wear long sleeves and long pants or skirts. Skip shorts and sleeveless tops. Ask whether women visitors should bring a headscarf.
- **Take off your shoes.** Leave them on the shelves by the prayer hall door. Slip-on shoes and clean socks make this easier.
- **Washing areas.** You may see people washing before prayer. This is _wudu_, the ritual washing. Visitors who aren't praying don't need to do it.
- **Men's and women's areas.** A mosque may have separate entrances or prayer areas for men and women.
- **During prayer.** Sit quietly toward the back and silence your phone. Don't walk in front of anyone who is praying. A group prayer takes about 5 to 10 minutes. The Friday midday prayer (_jumu'ah_) is longer because it includes a sermon.
- **Greetings.** You'll hear "_As-salamu alaykum_" (peace be upon you). The reply is "_Wa alaykum as-salam_" (and upon you be peace). Many Muslims shake hands only with people of the same gender, so let the other person go first.
- **Photos.** Ask before you take pictures.

## Becoming Muslim

A person becomes Muslim by believing the _shahada_, the declaration of faith, and saying it sincerely. It is the first of the Five Pillars.[^shahada]

<Shahada />

The first half says that only God deserves worship. The second accepts Muhammad as God's messenger, which means following the guidance he brought.

**You don't need witnesses.** Saying the shahada sincerely, even alone, is enough. Many people choose to say it at a mosque with others there, and some Islamic centers give a certificate. That is a choice, not a requirement.[^witnesses]

### After saying the shahada

Start by learning the daily prayers. Read the Qur'an in a translation you understand. If you have a mosque nearby, ask if it offers classes for new Muslims.

Want to talk first? You can [reach me on TikTok](https://go.alphabravomedia.co/islam-tiktok).

### What changes after you become Muslim

Becoming Muslim wipes out what came before. The Prophet ﷺ told Amr ibn al-As that Islam erases the sins that came before it.[^clean-slate] You do not carry your past into this.

It does not make life easy. Muslims still face hardship, stress and anxiety. The Qur'an says believers will be tested.[^test] The Prophet taught that the prophets were tried hardest, then those nearest to God.[^trials]

What changes is the guidance. The Qur'an covers worship, money, marriage, grief and fear. Muslims learn who God is through His names and attributes. They also learn through the prophets' stories. Those prophets were mocked, afraid and bereaved, and they kept going. Truly it is in the remembrance of God that hearts find peace.[^remembrance]

Some new Muslims feel isolated when family or friends do not share their faith. That is common. Find a local community early rather than waiting until you need one.

Islam teaches that every child is born with the natural inclination to worship one God, and that parents and upbringing shape the religion a person follows.[^fitrah] Muslims see that inclination as what a person returns to, not a claim about your family or your past.
