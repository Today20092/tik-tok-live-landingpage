I prayed and prayed and haven't seen it answered.

That was the exact sentence I typed when I started looking into this. I'd heard there was a hadith about it, and I wanted to know what it actually says and what it's supposed to mean.

This is the first of four posts that came out of that search. Each question led to the next one, so I'm sharing them in the order I asked them.

## The hadith I was thinking of

It turns out my sentence was almost word for word what the Prophet ﷺ described:

<HadithQuote hadith="muslim-2735c-impatience" />

What surprised me is that the hadith doesn't say it's wrong to feel this way, or even to notice that nothing has happened yet. It describes something specific: saying "I asked and it wasn't answered," getting worn out, and then *abandoning* du'a. That's the impatience it warns about.

So the hadith assumes that people will experience delay. It doesn't pretend that every du'a is answered on our schedule. It tells us what not to do with that delay: don't quit.

## What "answered" actually means

The next question I had was: what counts as being answered? Another narration gives three possibilities:

<HadithQuote hadith="adab-710-three-outcomes" />

A similar narration in at-Tirmidhi mentions the du'a being granted or an equal harm being turned away, and ends with the same reply: Allah has more.[^tirmidhi]

That changed how I look at my own situation. Only one of those three outcomes is something I can actually see. If a harm was turned away from me, I probably never knew it was coming. If the reward is being stored for the Hereafter, I won't see it in this life. So "I haven't seen it answered" doesn't mean it wasn't answered.

Allah also says He is near and that He responds:

<QuranVerse verse="2:186" />

In another verse, He says, "Call on Me and I will answer you."[^call]

## So how do I know when to stop asking?

This was my real question. If receiving the thing is the only answer I can be certain of, then isn't that the only time I'd know the du'a was answered? And if so, when do I stop asking for something specific?

I've come to understand it like this. Du'a isn't just a request waiting for a result. The Prophet ﷺ called it worship itself.[^worship] So the act of asking already counts, whether or not the specific thing arrives. That's why I don't think "enough time has passed" is a reason to stop.

The way I understand it now, a specific du'a changes when the situation itself changes:

- **I received it.** Then I thank Allah instead of asking for what I already have.
- **It becomes something I shouldn't ask for.** Both hadith above exclude asking for sin or cutting off family ties.
- **The matter is decided in the world.** The door clearly closes, and the du'a turns to asking for what is good for me instead.

What doesn't qualify is "I asked for a while and nothing happened, so Allah must have said no." That's the exact conclusion the hadith warns against.

## Times the texts point to

While reading, I also came across times that are specifically mentioned for du'a. A du'a made between the adhan and the iqamah is not rejected.[^adhan] And in the last third of the night, Allah asks, "Is there anyone to invoke Me, so that I may respond?"[^night]

I don't read these as a trick to make du'a work faster. For me they're an encouragement to keep asking, at the times I've been told to ask.

## What I'm taking from this

The hadith didn't promise me that I'll get what I asked for, when I want it. What it told me is what *not* to do with the wait. Don't turn the silence into a verdict, don't get worn out, and don't stop asking.

That led straight into my next question, which was about asking for something very specific, like a particular person in marriage.
